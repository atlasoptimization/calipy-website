01 / Sensor calibration

Is the signal changing, or is the sensor settling?

A sensor can report a changing value even when the physical quantity is stable. After power-up, its response may approach equilibrium while the measurement variation depends on the signal level.

m(t) = T∞ − ΔT exp(−t / τ)
y(t) ∼ Normal(m(t), σ(t))

The steady level T∞, initial deviation ΔT, and time constant τ answer different scientific questions. A noise model such as σ(t) = s0 + s1|m(t)| makes signal-dependent measurement uncertainty explicit.

What you can investigate

  • How much of the observed change is explained by a settling response?
  • Which parameters are identifiable from the available time range?
  • Does a constant-noise model explain the residuals, or is a signal-dependent law needed?

This modeling pattern is represented in the development introductory examples. The homepage plot is a separate illustrative simulation; it is not a fitted experimental result.

02 / Repeated measurements

Separate variation in the object from variation in the reading.

A set of parts can vary in their true properties, and the instrument can add further measurement noise. Treating every observation as unrelated can hide that distinction.

θj ∼ Normal(μ, σbetween)
yij ∼ Normal(θj, σmeasurement)

A hierarchical model gives each object its own latent level while describing the population they share. Repeated measurements can help distinguish variation between objects from within-object measurement variation.

What you can investigate

  • How much of the total variability belongs to the production process?
  • How precisely is each object’s level known?
  • Does your experiment contain enough repeated readings to distinguish the two sources?

Named dimensions make the roles of objects and repeated readings explicit. Priors, identifiability, and sampling design still require scientific judgment; a flexible model does not remove those decisions.

03 / Geodesy and metrology

Connect geometry to what the instrument actually observes.

Calibration often starts with a known geometric relationship and an imperfect instrument: an offset in a tape measurement, collimation error in a level, or angular errors in a total station.

observed reading = instrument response(geometry, parameters) + measurement variation

The CaliPy instrument-model paper introduces the library’s approach to composing probabilistic instrument models and includes examples with instrument effects, drift, and noise. The public documentation provides an engineering-geodesy example collection.

What you can investigate

  • Which instrument parameters explain a systematic residual pattern?
  • How do uncertainties in inputs contribute to the final result?
  • Which observation configurations can separate competing effects?

A model needs evidence, not just a fit.

A result should make sense numerically and structurally. Check the model’s scientific assumptions, observation handling, dimension roles, parameter meaning, and inference diagnostics. Where possible, compare against a closed-form solution, a simulation study, or an independent reference.
